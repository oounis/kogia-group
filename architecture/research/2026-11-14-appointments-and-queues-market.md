# Appointments and queues: market research, Libya first

Date: 2026-11-14
Scope: Libya (first commercial market), then Arabic North Africa and international products.
Companion file: `2026-11-11-municipalities-and-public-bodies.md`. That file covers municipal law, who issues which document, procurement and Dawlati in detail. This file does not repeat it; it only adds what concerns appointments and queues.

**How to read the labels**

- **VERIFIED**: the source page was opened and read on 2026-11-14.
- **REPORTED**: seen only as a search-result snippet, a secondary article, or a page that could not be opened today.
- **ASSUMPTION**: reasoning by the author, no source.
- **No evidence found**: searched in Arabic, English and French; nothing usable came back. This does not prove the thing does not exist. Much of Libyan public communication happens on Facebook pages, which desk research cannot search reliably.

Numbers in square brackets point to the source list at the end.

## 1. Libya: how appointments and queues work today

### (b) Municipalities

- No Libyan municipality with an online appointment or queue-ticket system was found. **No evidence found.**
- Tripoli Centre municipality announced on 19 April 2026 the start of development of "BaladiyaBOT", a digital assistant, under the German GIZ municipal programme [11]. REPORTED.
- The unified municipal structure requires electronic systems in the licences office and the citizen service office, and the mayor holds financial powers (companion file, sections A1.1 and A1.4). No queue or walk-in data for these offices was found.

### (d) Banks

- Queues are well documented but their cause is cash shortage, not poor flow management. Reports describe near-daily queues outside banks [16], people waiting hours at ATMs to withdraw LD 1,000 in three operations [17], and waits of days in Tripoli in 2019 [18]. REPORTED.
- In 2017 a Tripoli man built a small business standing in bank queues for clients at LD 50 a time [19]. VERIFIED.
- In August 2026 the Central Bank set monthly cash withdrawal ceilings of LD 3,000 (LD 4,000 in the south), split between counters and ATMs [20]. REPORTED.
- Any Libyan bank offering branch appointment booking or naming a queue-system supplier: **no evidence found**. (Egyptian banks do offer branch booking [21], REPORTED.)
- Who decides: bank management under Central Bank supervision; a 2025 Central Bank circular imposes a data-protection regime on the banking sector (title only read) [22]. REPORTED.

### (e) Embassies and consulates

This segment is already served by outsourcing firms.

- Italy: Almaviva runs visa centres in Tripoli, Benghazi and Misrata with its own appointment system (since March 2023) [23]. VERIFIED. During the change of contractor the online booking was suspended and requests went by email [24]. VERIFIED.
- Malta uses VFS Global in Tripoli [25]; Greece uses Global Visa Center World with VFS as subcontractor [26]. REPORTED.
- Italy's offices issued more than 15,000 visas in Libya in 2024 [27]. REPORTED.
- Libyan missions abroad use the MOFA form described in (a) [6].

### (f) Schools and universities

- Libyan International University (private, Benghazi) launched automated registration for 2025-2026 and requires digital processing of student transactions from March 2026 [28]. REPORTED.
- Sebha University asks new students to pre-register online and bring a reference number [29]. REPORTED.
- University of Tripoli's registrar page gives rules only, no online service [30]. VERIFIED.
- Public schools (parent appointments, enrolment queues): **no evidence found.**

### (g) Telecoms, utilities, private business

- Operators push self-service apps to avoid branch visits (LTT Life, Almadar app with a branch map) [31]. Almadar deployed a customer-service chatbot on Messenger and web chat in 2024 [32]. REPORTED.
- Queue or appointment systems in telecom, electricity or water service centres: **no evidence found.**

## 5. Gaps and opportunities (ANALYSIS, not fact)

Everything in this section is the author's reasoning from the findings above.

1. **Arabic first, three languages.** None of the international pricing pages read today states Arabic or right-to-left support. Skiplino has an Arabic site; Vezeeta is Arabic but is a marketplace. A product that is natively Arabic RTL with English and French is a real differentiator against the cloud tools. It is weaker against Wavetec, whose language support is unknown.
2. **Works when the network or power is down.** Blackouts and internet disruptions are documented [38][39]. Cloud-only tools (Qminder, Waitwhile, QLess) stop when the link drops. A local-first front desk that keeps issuing and calling tickets offline and syncs later fits the documented conditions.
3. **On-premises or private hosting.** There is no localisation law [42], but public bodies and banks have their own circulars [22][43] whose content is unread. Offering local hosting removes the question. Only Skiplino and Qmatic clearly offer it among the products read.
4. **Per-service queues with a public screen, on ordinary hardware.** Qminder needs Apple devices; Wavetec sells kiosks. A browser-based display on any TV and a ticket printed on a cheap thermal printer, or shown on the phone by QR, lowers entry cost.
5. **QR walk-in ticket without an app.** Qmatic and Wavetec already do this, so it is expected, not novel. Facebook and Messenger reach are very high [33]; WhatsApp reach is unknown. SMS is available but sender names must be alphanumeric [37].
6. **Pricing.** USD 299 to 1,149 per month is out of reach for a private clinic, and foreign-currency subscriptions are hard to pay from Libya (ASSUMPTION about payment difficulty). A dinar-priced, yearly licence sold through the local partner fits better.
7. **Do not sell "online booking" alone to the state.** National bodies already have, or announce, their own booking sites (passports, MOFA, CRA). The open space is the on-site queue and the many bodies with nothing.
8. **Fairness is a selling point.** Paid queue-jumping is documented for passports [1] and bank queues [19]. A visible first-come order with an audit trail answers a known complaint.

## 6. Most promising first customer segments in Libya (ANALYSIS)

| Rank | Segment | Evidence behind it | Main doubt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Private clinics and polyclinics (masahhat) in Tripoli, Benghazi, Misrata | Several booking apps exist, so the need is recognised [12][13]; they serve the patient side, not the front desk; no local clinic-queue vendor found; one owner decides (ASSUMPTION) | How clinics book today is unknown; app founders report user resistance [12] |
| 2 | Public health centres, laboratories, diagnostic and vaccination centres | A public health body in Tripoli bought a full queue system in 2026 [35], so budget and need exist | Wavetec is already in; procurement under Decision 600 |
| 3 | Municipal citizen-service and licences offices | Legal duty to use electronic systems (companion file); GIZ-funded digital work in municipalities [11]; newly elected councils | No queue data; no municipality has bought such a system as far as found |
| 4 | Private universities and training institutes | LIMU is moving all student transactions to digital [28] | Small number of buyers; may build in-house |
| 5 | Bank branches | Best-documented queues in the country [16][17][18][19] | Queues come from cash shortage, which software does not fix; heavy vendor rules [22]; long sales cycle |
| 6 | Local branches of the passports authority and civil registry | Very high volumes [3]; documented crowding and paid shortcuts [1] | Central ministries own the systems; only an on-site queue layer is realistic |
| 7 | Telecom, electricity and water service centres | Operators are investing in customer-service tools [32] | No evidence of queue pain or of any purchase |

Not recommended as a first target: visa centres (already held by VFS, Almaviva and GVCW [23][25][26]) and public schools (no evidence of any kind).

### Questions for the Libyan partner (desk research cannot answer these)

1. How do patients book at ten named clinics today: phone, walk-in, Facebook, WhatsApp? Who answers, and how many no-shows are there?
2. Which of the doctor apps (Speetar, Tibbi, Wijhatak) do clinics really use, and do clinics pay for them?
3. Who sells and installs ticket machines and queue screens in Libya now? Brand names, prices, where they are installed (banks, clinics, telecom shops).
4. Who was the local agent, if any, in the Wavetec project at the National Centre for Disease Control, and how was it bought?
5. Is the passports booking site live today, and at what address? Is the civil registry ID-card booking real, given that cra.gov.ly is a placeholder?
6. Do any banks already give numbered tickets or booked slots on cash days?
7. Is WhatsApp or Messenger the normal channel between a business and its customers? Would people accept a ticket by WhatsApp?
8. What does a local SMS cost through Libyana and Almadar, and can a company register a sender name directly?
9. Do public bodies demand that data stay on servers inside Libya? What do the GIA circular 1 of 2024 and the Central Bank circular 18 of 2025 require from a supplier?
10. Under Decision 600 of 2024, up to what amount can a municipality or health centre buy directly without a tender? Is the 2024 regulation applied in the east?
11. What yearly price in dinars would a clinic owner and a mayor each consider reasonable, and how would they pay?
12. How many hours a day do clinics and offices in the target cities lose power or internet, and do they have generators and a local network?
